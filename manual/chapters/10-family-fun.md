# Why This Is Family Fun

Most dry-fire practice is solitary and silent. This is the opposite, and nearly
every decision in the app points the same way.

**Two people, one screen, no equipment to divide.** Press T and the screen splits.
There is nothing to pair, nothing to log in to and nothing to configure per
player. Whoever is holding the second pistol is in the game.

**A round is short.** Seventeen rounds takes twenty or thirty seconds. Nobody
waits long for a turn, and a whole evening is a stack of rounds rather than one
long session.

**The numbers show their working.** The efficiency has its arithmetic printed
underneath it and the score shows both the factors it is made of, so nobody has to
take the app's word for anything. Points win the round, the careful shooter and
the fast shooter each get a column to point at, and what is left to argue about is
which number matters -- which is the good kind of argument.

**The sides swap every round.** The result cannot be blamed on which half of the
screen anybody had.

**The drills explain themselves.** A ball you have to hit, targets you have to
clear, a figure that pops up. Nobody has to read this manual to play, which is
the only honest test of a game.

**The targets are drawings, not abstractions.** Fifteen hand-drawn figures, each
with scoring rings printed on it, plus a filmed soldier who takes four good hits
and goes down. A child who is bored by a circle is not bored by a zombie.

**It is entirely offline.** No account, no network, no subscription, nothing
installed, nothing phoned home. Unzip a folder and shoot.

## What you hear

Every registered shot plays a gunshot. That bang is the app's way of saying it
saw the trigger pull, which is why nothing is ever played *instead* of it: an
effect follows a millisecond later and never replaces it.

| Sound | When |
|--------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| Gunshot | Any registered shot |
| Bubble pop | A ring target is hit, and a pull on an empty magazine |
| Glass breaking | A ball bursts |
| Notification | A target appears in Reaction |
| Shrill horn | A figure appears |
| A grunt, or a banshee | A figure goes down |

The app also speaks: it announces each drill by name, warns you to hold fire
while it is measuring, and tells you when it believes the camera has moved. The
lines are recorded in three different voices and one is chosen at random, so the
app does not sound like one announcer reading a script. Nothing is spoken within
three seconds of a trigger pull, except the warnings that must be heard
mid-string.

**+** and **-** set the volume, and the setting is remembered. The floor is one
per cent rather than zero: a key that could silence the app entirely would bring
back a fault that took three days to find, when every shot was being played
correctly into a volume slider somebody had dragged to the bottom.
